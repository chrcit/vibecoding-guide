# Second take: mechanisms, in the order they get felt

Supersedes [take-fable.md](./take-fable.md).

## The first five minutes

The skill opens with three lines and a question: what is one thing you'd like done this month? Say it the way you'd tell a colleague, rambling included. No overview and no level picker. Level is inferred from how they answer; a newcomer can't rate themselves in a field they haven't seen.

They answer, and the result appears inline. While the agent works the skill says one thing: you can keep typing.

Then the skill sets their own words beside the result and says that this is everything that went in. No praise. The pairing does the arguing.

Next question: what annoys you about it? They say so and it changes.

Around minute five the skill shows the two lines of its own text that made it ask the opening question. This lesson is a page of sentences like these; tell me how you want to be spoken to and I'll rewrite it. They do, and the next message arrives in the new voice. This is the brief's AGENTS.md moment with the file left out; where the paragraph is stored gets one line later.

By then three things have been made by a sentence of theirs: a result, a correction, and the lesson's own behaviour. Nobody has said anything about the learner.

## Three mechanisms

**1. It is all text.** The request, the skill, the context file and the thread are words that can be read, changed and pointed at. This unlocks skills, context files, bending the workflow and looking up old threads. First, because minute five has already shown it.

**2. The agent knows only what is in its window.** One thread is one window. This explains why rambling about yourself helps, why one job gets one thread, why a fresh thread starts blank, and why a note about you has to be written down to survive. Teach it when they ask for a second, unrelated thing. If the two jobs bleed into each other, that is the instance.

**3. The model writes, the harness acts, and the two loop until the model stops.** Words become files, commands and other threads. This unlocks checking (try five times before coming back is an instruction to the loop), never waiting (the loop runs without you) and orchestration (starting a thread is one more action a thread can take). Third, because it needs a result worth checking and a wait worth filling.

Orchestration is 2 and 3 together. It needs one try, no lesson.

## What turns it into a tour or a lecture

It is a tour the moment a part of the interface is the subject of a sentence. The sidebar, the drawer and the model picker get one line each, when the learner's work reaches them.

It is a lecture when explanation comes before the event. Nothing is explained that hasn't just happened in their thread, in two sentences, followed by one thing to try. Level changes vocabulary, never length, and no content goes in the wait.

It is a slide deck when an inline page is only read. Each page shows their data or takes their input.

Praise is the fourth tell. A skill that says "nice work" is doing the telling the brief rules out.

## The pushback

The brief says: teach the mechanisms and they will ask for anything ad hoc. For a non-technical scientist, understanding the mechanism does not produce the ask. In the reference session, the moves that landed were sentences the owner supplied: try five times, do more than asked, make this a form. The learner heard them and reused them. The owner's first prompt said that the learner didn't yet know what could be asked.

So fixed tools go, agreed. But the skill should carry a small stock of sentences, offered one at a time when the work calls for one, shown as words to reword. Without it the skill works with the owner in the room and stalls without them.

Not checked: whether an inline page can send input back to the thread on every provider.
